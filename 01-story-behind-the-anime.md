# The Story Behind the Anime

*A study of how Kōhei Horikoshi's My Hero Academia became one of the defining shōnen franchises of the 2010s.*

## 1. The Creator: Kōhei Horikoshi

Kōhei Horikoshi was born on **August 20, 1986** in **Aichi Prefecture**, Japan. He grew up as an avid reader of manga and a devoted fan of superhero fiction — an unusual combination in Japan at the time, where the American comic book tradition was far less visible than domestic manga.

### Influences

Horikoshi has been openly enthusiastic about the sources that shaped *My Hero Academia*:

- **Akira Toriyama** — *Dragon Ball* and especially *Dr. Slump*. Horikoshi has cited Toriyama's clean, energetic linework and his talent for expressive, instantly readable faces as a foundational influence.
- **Masashi Kishimoto** — *Naruto*. Horikoshi admired how Kishimoto balanced a huge cast with genuine emotional through-lines. He has also spoken about how *Naruto*'s treatment of a lonely, ostracised protagonist informed his own approach to Izuku Midoriya.
- **American superhero comics** — particularly **Spider-Man** and **Batman**. Spider-Man is the single most important reference: a teenage hero who is clumsy, self-doubting, funny, and morally uncompromising. Horikoshi has said he wanted to draw a manga that captured the *feeling* of American superhero comics while obeying the structural rules of *Shōnen Jump*.
- **Kaiju and tokusatsu** — the giant-monster and special-effects tradition (Godzilla, Ultraman, Kamen Rider) feeds directly into the design of the Nomu and the general spectacle of super-powered combat.

### Two Failures Before the Breakthrough

Horikoshi's road to success was not straightforward. He serialised two earlier works in *Weekly Shōnen Jump*, both of which were cancelled relatively quickly:

| Work | Year | Fate |
|---|---|---|
| **Ōmagadoki Zoo** | 2010–2011 | Cancelled; 3 volumes |
| **Barrage** (*Sense no Kyū* / 戦星のバルジ) | 2012 | Cancelled; 2 volumes |

This context matters enormously for understanding the series. Horikoshi has spoken candidly about the pressure of a third attempt: in *Jump*'s brutally competitive reader-survey system, a series that fails to place well is axed within months. *My Hero Academia* was, in his own framing, his last real chance.

He prepared it carefully. Before serialisation he published a one-shot, and he developed the concept with a specific structural question in mind: **what happens to a society where superpowers are ordinary?** Most superhero fiction treats powers as rare and exceptional. Horikoshi inverted this — in his world, roughly **80% of humanity** possesses a power. That inversion is the engine of the entire series.

## 2. Publication History

*My Hero Academia* debuted in **Issue 32 of *Weekly Shōnen Jump*, released July 7, 2014**.

The series became a cornerstone of the magazine's 2010s line-up, alongside *One Piece*, *Haikyuu!!* and later *Demon Slayer* and *Jujutsu Kaisen*. It sustained strong reader-survey rankings throughout its run, allowing Horikoshi to plan a long-form narrative with a definite ending — a relative rarity in *Jump*, where many series are extended past their natural conclusion or cancelled abruptly.

**The series concluded on August 5, 2024**, after exactly ten years of serialisation and 430 chapters.

### The Ending and the Epilogue

The final stretch of publication deserves a note, because it is often confused:

- **Chapter 429** concluded the *Final War* arc and, in Horikoshi's own view, the main story of Izuku Midoriya.
- **Chapter 430** served as a time-skip epilogue set eight years later.
- **Chapter 431** — a **38-page epilogue** included in volume 42 — extended that time skip further and provided additional closure.

Because chapters 430 and 431 were released as extra content rather than as part of the main serialisation, some readers questioned whether they counted as canon. In the afterword to volume 42, **Horikoshi explicitly confirmed that the epilogue is canon**, comparing the final two chapters to "unlockable content in a video game" — not part of the central plot, but genuine and official. He closed by stating that with chapter 431, he was "releasing" his characters from their story.

## 3. From Page to Screen: The Anime Adaptation

The anime adaptation was produced by **Studio Bones** (*Fullmetal Alchemist*, *Mob Psycho 100*, *Carole & Tuesday*), a studio with a long reputation for high-quality action animation.

### Key Production Staff

| Role | Name |
|---|---|
| **Director** | Kenji Nagasaki |
| **Series composition / script** | Yōsuke Kuroda |
| **Character design** | Yoshihiko Umakoshi |
| **Music** | Yuki Hayashi |
| **Studio** | Bones |
| **Supervisor (films)** | Kōhei Horikoshi |

The consistency of this core team across nearly a decade is a major reason the adaptation feels coherent. **Yuki Hayashi's** score — brassy, orchestral, and deliberately heroic — is widely credited with elevating the material; themes such as "You Say Run" have become iconic in their own right.

In Japan the series aired on the JNN network (MBS) for Season 1 and on NNS (ytv) from Season 2 onward. Internationally it became one of the flagship titles of the streaming era, distributed by Crunchyroll and, in earlier years, Funimation.

**The Final Season concluded the adaptation on December 13, 2025** — an eight-season, nine-year-and-eight-month run. Notably, all eleven episodes of the Final Season earned ratings above 9.0 on IMDb, making it the first full-length anime season to achieve that record.

## 4. The Premise: An Inverted Superhero World

The core conceit can be stated in a single paragraph:

> Roughly four generations before the story begins, a glowing baby is born in China with a superpower. Within decades, powers — called **Quirks** — spread to about **80% of the human population**. Society does not collapse; it adapts. A professional licensing system is created, **Heroes** become a legal and celebrated occupation ranked on a public "Billboard Chart", hero agencies operate like businesses, and schools such as **U.A. High School** train the next generation. Crime adapts too: **Villains** use their Quirks for profit and ideology, while unlicensed **Vigilantes** operate outside the law.

Against this backdrop, **Izuku Midoriya** is born into the powerless 20%. He is bullied, written off, and told by his own teachers that his dream is unrealistic. The story opens not with an action sequence but with a boy who has already been told he cannot do the thing he loves most.

When he meets **All Might** — the Symbol of Peace and holder of the transferable Quirk **One For All** — he is initially rejected. It is only after Midoriya charges a monster to save his tormentor, Katsuki Bakugo, that All Might recognises the quality he actually requires: the instinct to move without thinking when someone else is in danger.

All Might's words on passing the torch are the thesis statement of the series:

> *"Young man, you too can become a hero."*

### One For All and All For One

The two central powers of the series are deliberate mirrors:

- **One For All** — a stockpiled power that is *given away*. Nine wielders, each contributing to the next. It represents inheritance, trust, and self-sacrifice.
- **All For One** — a power that *takes* Quirks from others and can *impose* Quirks on them. It represents accumulation, domination, and the reduction of other people to resources.

The entire conflict of *My Hero Academia* is, at its philosophical core, these two ideas fighting each other.

## 5. Themes

### What Does It Mean to Be a Hero?

The series refuses a simple answer. Different characters embody different definitions:

- **All Might** — the Symbol of Peace: a hero whose *presence* reassures, who must always smile so others feel safe.
- **Stain, the Hero Killer** — argues that modern heroes have become celebrities motivated by fame and money rather than self-sacrifice. He is a villain, but his critique lands, and it is treated seriously rather than dismissed.
- **Endeavor** — a hero who achieved the #2 rank through obsession and cruelty, and whose redemption arc is one of the most uncomfortable in the series.
- **Deku** — a hero who "meddles when he doesn't need to." His definition is ultimately the one the narrative endorses, but the series also shows its cost: in later arcs, his attempt to protect everyone by working alone is portrayed as a genuine mistake, not a triumph.

### Failure Is Structural, Not Personal

One of the most serious themes is that **hero society produces its own villains.** Several antagonists — Tomura Shigaraki, Dabi/Toya Todoroki, Himiko Toga, Lady Nagant — are people whom the system failed, ignored, or actively harmed. The narrative consistently asks the reader to hold two things at once: these characters commit terrible acts, *and* society contributed to making them.

### Self-Sacrifice and Its Cost

Deku breaks his own body repeatedly in the early arcs. This is played for dramatic effect at first, but Horikoshi gradually reframes it — the series becomes increasingly critical of a hero culture that expects people to destroy themselves for others, and of a protagonist who cannot accept help.

### Inheritance and Legacy

Nearly every significant power in the series is inherited, taught, stolen, or passed on. Mentorship — All Might to Deku, Gran Torino, Endeavor's work-study, Aizawa's class — is the actual mechanism of progress.

## 6. Cultural Impact and Legacy

### Commercial Success

By April 2024 the manga had surpassed **100 million copies in circulation worldwide**, placing it among the best-selling manga series in history. Volume releases regularly topped Japan's Oricon charts.

### Critical Recognition

- Nominated for the **Manga Taishō** award.
- Winner of **Best Manga at the Harvey Awards (2019)** — one of the most prestigious American comics honours.
- Repeatedly ranked among the top-selling and most-streamed anime titles globally.

### Expansion of the Franchise

The success of the main series spawned an unusually large extended universe:

| Spin-off | Format | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **My Hero Academia: Vigilantes** | Manga by Hideyuki Furuhashi & Betten Court | Prequel focusing on unlicensed vigilantes; ran 2016–2022, 15 volumes. Anime adaptation premiered April 2025. |
| **My Hero Academia Smash!!** | Four-panel gag manga by Hirofumi Neda | Ran November 2015 – November 2017, 5 volumes. |
| **My Hero Academia: Team-Up Missions** | Manga by Yōkō Akiyama | Ran 2019–2024. |
| **My Hero Academia: School Briefs** | Light novels by Anri Takashi | Prose side stories. |
| **My Hero Academia: Illegals** | — | Alternate English title used for *Vigilantes*. |

The franchise also extended into video games (*One's Justice*, *One's Justice 2*, *Ultra Rumble*, *Spark!!*), stage plays, and character merchandise that became a global phenomenon.

### Why It Endured

*My Hero Academia* arrived at a specific moment: Western superhero cinema was at its commercial peak, but Japanese manga had not produced a mainstream superhero title of comparable reach since the genre's earlier experiments. Horikoshi filled that gap — and did so with a distinctly Japanese sensibility, treating heroism as a *profession* embedded in institutions, schools, bureaucracy and public opinion rather than as a secret identity.

Its most lasting contribution may be structural. By making superpowers ordinary, Horikoshi was able to explore questions that traditional superhero fiction rarely addresses: how do you regulate power? Who decides who gets to be a hero? What happens to the children whose abilities are frightening rather than impressive? Those questions kept the series relevant far beyond its action sequences.
