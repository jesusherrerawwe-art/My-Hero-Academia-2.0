## 1. Quick identity card

| | |
|---|---|
| **Name** | El Charro Negro ("The Black Charro / Black Horseman") |
| **Type** | Spectral horseman; devil's collector; damned soul; (in some variants) just guardian spirit |
| **Domain** | Lonely roads, crossroads, town outskirts — at nightfall/twilight or under a full moon |
| **Appearance** | Tall, elegant, handsome man in an *impeccable black charro suit* with **gold/silver embroidery**, wide-brimmed sombrero shadowing his face, mounted on a huge jet-black horse whose **eyes burn like coals** and whose nostrils snort steam |
| **Face** | Usually hidden by the hat brim; at the moment of revelation it is **a skull** — or he has no face at all |
| **Method** | Appears to lone travelers; politely offers gold coins from a small waist-pouch, or a ride on his horse |
| **The rule** | **Accept the coins or mount the horse, and your luck — your soul — is surrendered.** The gold later turns to dry leaves, manure, or ash |
| **Fate** | Condemned to wander, collecting for the Devil, forever searching for someone to **take his place** |
| **Core sin** | Greed / ambition; in moral-enforcer variants he also punishes drunkards, adulterers, disobedient children, and the arrogant |
| **Wards** | Garlic (Mixtec tradition); in the main telling, even a private chapel full of crosses does **not** save the pact-maker — only refusal protects you |

**Headline:** this is Mexico's **Faust** — a pact-with-the-devil legend wearing the most iconic, dignified national costume. The horror wears formalwear and asks politely. *Consent is the entire mechanism.*

## 2. The canonical popular telling (synthesized)

A poor, ambitious young campesino is obsessed with looking wealthy — fine clothes even when he has nothing to eat. In despair he invokes the Devil (some say Lucifer, Mephistopheles, or Mammon) and trades his soul for riches, beauty, and elegance. He enjoys it for years, forgets the bargain, then remembers in terror. He builds chapels and forges crosses around his ranch; finally he flees at night on his best horse with a bag of gold. The Devil catches him, and his flesh withers away to the bone in seconds — but he is allowed to keep the horse. Condemned, he rides the backroads forever: polished, courteous, offering other travelers the same bargain, because he can only rest if **another greedy soul agrees to take his place**.

Cholula, Puebla preserves a famous anchored sighting (c. 1930, gatekeeper Francisco by the Río Atoyac / old Puente de México), but the legend is told across Jalisco, Guanajuato, Michoacán, Puebla, Hidalgo, Tlaxcala, Veracruz, Morelos, Xochimilco/Tlalpan, and beyond — it even migrated to Colombia, Venezuela, and the U.S. Southwest via migration and cinema.

**Recurring sensory details:** a chill with no source; hoofbeats or little bells whose sound cuts off mid-echo; a horse that moves almost soundlessly; extreme politeness; the hat brim that never lifts until it's too late; he avoids big cities.

## 3. The deep roots (this is where it gets powerful)

The legend is a **colonial syncretism sandwich**, and each layer gives us different story material:

### 3a. Pre-Hispanic: the guardian of the land
- Before the Conquest, nocturnal spirits in indigenous lore were not devils — they were **guardians of territory, crops, treasure, mines, and animals**, defending them from the greed of outsiders.
- **Mixtec — "Señor del Cerro" (Lord of the Hill):** a white, tall horseman (already wearing the colonizer's image) who lives on the mountain, guards buried treasure, **punishes deforestation and greed**, and must be asked permission with offerings (cigarettes, mezcal, food). **Garlic wards him off.** A highway project in the Sierra Norte de Puebla reportedly stopped work until his permission was obtained.
- **Mazatec — "San Martín de Caballero":** by day a folk saint prayed to for money (alfalfa offered to his horse); by night he is the owner of all lands and mountains, white, greeting people in Castilian. To receive wealth: sexual abstinence, offerings of cocoa or turkey, lead his horse home **by the tail, and speak to no one for four years**. Break the vow and you die instantly; he takes body and soul to work for him forever.
- **Wixárika (Huichol) — Tamatsi Teiwari Yuawi, "Our Big Brother the Dark-Blue Mestizo":** the single richest figure. A cattle/ranching god who endlessly transgresses and enriches himself *without reciprocity*; identified across regions with San Cristóbal, with Santiago (the saint-knight on horseback), and even with the dead Christ. He is **more powerful than the indigenous gods themselves, but he is a despot, a collector, and he knows no forgiveness** — the colonizer made into a deity out of trauma.
- **Mexica duality:** Tezcatlipoca (lord of night, sorcery, jaguars, destiny, the smoking mirror) vs. Quetzalcóatl; darkness was understood as a **necessary half of cosmic balance**, a constant temptation tied to human passion. Catholicism later re-graded that duality as God vs. Lucifer.

### 3b. Colonial: the guardian becomes the Devil
Spanish evangelization didn't erase native deities — it **saintified or demonized** them. The mounted, wealthy, land-owning outsider (the hacienda overseer, the bullfighter, the cattle baron) became the devil's face; selling your labor and land to the hacienda was understood folklorically as "selling your soul." The modern Charro Negro therefore carries **two opposite charges at once**: a punisher of greed *and* the predator who feeds it; the colonizer's violence *and* the colonized's warning system.

### 3c. Historical: real charros, corridos, and cinema
- Charros of 18th–19th c. Jalisco were skilled horsemen — arrieros, rancheros, hacienda men, sometimes bandits — in embroidered suits with silver botonadura. The costume already meant *wealth, pride, and authority.*
- The legend as most Mexicans know it consolidated around the **1920s** (some scholarship dates it there as an explicit indigenous/European syncretism), spread through oral tradition, the corrido *"El Charro Negro"* (the woman invited onto the horse who never returns), and Golden Age cinema and radio (Jorge Negrete, Pedro Infante, Vicente Fernández).
- It never died: corridos tumbados (Natanael Cano, Junior H), tattoos, altars beside Catholic saints, alleged sighting videos — he remains a live symbol of *elegant rebellion and fatal temptation.*

## 4. Pop-culture footprint (know what's already out there)

- ***Macario*** (B. Traven's 1953 novel; the legendary 1960 film, first Mexican film nominated for the Oscar): the Devil appears to Macario as a **handsome, richly dressed charro scattering silver** — the defining literary image of the Charro Negro as temptation.
- ***La Leyenda del Charro Negro*** (Ánima Estudios, 2018; fifth *Las Leyendas* film, voiced by Erick Elías with a norteño accent): he grants **any favor in apparent exchange for a few coins**, really contracting souls; rules an underworld; tries to possess the hero. A sequel, ***La venganza del Charro Negro*** (8th franchise film), is the 2026 follow-up.
- Corridos, lucha libre imagery, TV appearances, and recurring "sightings" (e.g., a much-shared April 2025 purported surveillance clip of a mounted figure vanishing).
- **IP note for the contest:** the *legend* is public-domain Mexican folklore — nobody owns El Charro Negro. Specific film/novel *versions* are copyrighted; our interpretation must be our own (which it already is — calavera face + scarlet identity).

## 5. The motif bank (raw material for Quirk mechanics / techniques / scenes)

1. **The polite bargain** — he offers, never forces; power moves through *accepted terms* (contract/consent Quirk — almost unused in MHA, extremely fresh).
2. **The gold that becomes trash** — granted power with a hidden cost; blessings that reverse.
3. **The chain of succession** — "find someone to take your place"; a cursed chain of holders across generations.
4. **The collector who knows no forgiveness** — debts, accounts, ledgers; a demonic *cobrador*.
5. **The mount** — black horse, fire eyes, soundless hooves, steam (a ride you cannot dismount from).
6. **Thresholds** — crossroads, twilight, lonely roads, bridges, gates, rivers; he appears at *moments and places of decision.*
7. **The reveal** — hat lifts; skull beneath; courtesy peeling into the grave.
8. **The punisher of vice** — drunkards, adulterers, the greedy, arrogant, and cruel; a vigilante moralist in some tellings.
9. **The treasure-guardian variant** — riddles, bets, and trials instead of straight soul-theft (Oaxaca/Chiapas).
10. **Sound cues** — spurs, bells, hoofbeats that stop mid-ring; silence as his entrance.
11. **Wards and taboos** — garlic; silence for four years; never take the coin; never mount up.
12. **The color split** — he is *Negro* (black, funerary, hidden); our hero is **Escarlata** (scarlet — liturgical, living, passionate, blood of the heart).

## 6. Hana's analytical notes — how this detonates inside MHA 🤓

*(Interpretations and questions, not decisions — Kevin is the architect.)*

### 6a. The skull face is now explained by the myth
The legend's single scariest beat is **the hat lifting to show the skull.** Jesús didn't get a mask — he appears to have been **born inside the reveal.** The monster face the legend hides until the final second is the face he has worn since age four. The Día de Muertos calavera (Mexico's loving, laughing death) and the Charro Negro skull (death as debt-collector) are **two opposing Mexican skull symbolisms fused on one boy's face.** That is a thesis statement, not a design.

### 6b. The 50/50 Quirk premise fits MHA's frontier science
"50% biological, 50% mythological" is not even a contradiction in MHA terms — the world already has Quirk Singularity theory, the will of One For All's vestiges, the soul-transfer mechanics of Eri and Overhaul, and the *literal demon-form* of a Quirk like Dark Shadow. A **"mythic-type" Quirk factor that pattern-matches itself onto a living folk legend** (the story itself trying to happen through him) is the kind of unprecedented case Recovery Girl's generation couldn't diagnose and Present Mic's generation would name badly. It also gives an organic *drawback*: myths have rules, and the older half of his power may be bound to the legend's rules (bargains, thresholds, sunset, debt).

### 6c. The terrifying AFO/OFA mirror — read this twice
- **All For One is the Charro Negro.** He is the elegant, immortal collector who appears at people's lowest points and *offers* them what they crave in exchange for what makes them themselves — then keeps the stolen goods forever and forgives nothing.
- **One For All is the chain curse.** "Another must take your place" — a power passed down a line of holders, each inheriting the debt of the last, and the current holder is expected to be consumed by it.
- Jesús carrying a mythological Quirk built on **bargains and succession** walks into Class 1-A as a *living third answer* to both: the boy who knows the Devil's contract-language because part of him is related to it, and who is trying to **break the chain instead of passing it on.** This is potentially the cleanest possible thematic engine for "Jesús becomes the true protagonist while Deku stays present": Deku inherited a story; Jesús is refusing one. *(Subject to Kevin's actual Quirk reveal — flagging the alignment because it is enormous.)*

### 6d. The temptation route: Stain
Folk Charro Negro is often a *moral enforcer* who hounds the greedy and cruel. In MHA, that logic dead-ends at **Stain's ideology** (purge the false heroes; violence as accounting). Jesús may have to consciously reject the legend's oldest behavior pattern — *collecting debts* — in favor of heroism that frees debtors. A villain who literally embodies the unbroken Charro Negro line (a mentor? a blood relative? the Quirk's prior "holder"?) would be his dark mirror.

### 6e. Differentiation watch-list
- **Ghost Rider (Marvel):** pact-with-the-devil + flaming skull + black rider is a famous surface combo. We differentiate with: no Hell/Punisher iconography, no chains; Mexican folk-Catholic framework; **consent/bargain mechanics** over brute hellfire; mariachi/charro identity; scarlet not black; face-as-birth-mutation; sound/music motifs.
- **Tokoyami / Dark Shadow:** the black-red aura is close. Hard mechanical + visual differentiator required when the Quirk is revealed (the aura could, for example, be *mane/tail/hoof-shaped* or *coin/spark-shaped* rather than a clawed shadow familiar).
- **The Ánima film Charro Negro:** a charming soul-contractor with red tones — avoid duplicating his underworld-ruler characterization and coin-flip presentation beat-for-beat.
- **Dabi/AFO villain palette:** black-and-red reads "villain" in Japan — which is, of course, the *point* of Jesús's discrimination arc (see design notes on heteromorph profiling).

### 6f. Why "Escarlata" and not "Negro"
His costume retains the legend's black-and-gold silhouette, but his **hero name claims a different color.** Scarlet is the red of cardinals and the Sacred Heart, of *grito* passion, of blood that is alive. The cape lining is red: open the cloak and the black myth is lined in life. He wears the Charro's image and answers to a different name — **he looks like the story everyone fears and behaves like the mercy that story never offered.** That's not just a hero name; it's a character arc compressed into two words.
